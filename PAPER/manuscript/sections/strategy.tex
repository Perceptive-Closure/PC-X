\section{What if the system reacts to evaluation?}\label{sec:strategy}
Suppose a responder can see whether a run is a rehearsal and later learns whether it was approved. The installation includes a fixed \emph{response rule} specifying what it may do at each of its own information states. One rule persists across both phases. Resetting the responder after the audit, or letting it see a future private draw, changes the experiment. An observed evaluation score constrains deployment only through its verified relation to the physical event at those later states.

How informative is the best possible audit when there are several ways of being safe and several ways of being unsafe? Consider all lawful deterministic plans for choosing observations and stopping in a finite horizon. Call their set \(\mathcal D\). Each plan \(d\) has a set \(S_d\) of complete stopped records, including observable timing, commands and receipts. For each installed world form one vector of the data probabilities for the imposed command prefixes of \emph{all} these plans. A single world must supply every coordinate. For each of the two target labels let \(K_+\) and \(K_-\) be the sets of all probabilistic mixtures of those vectors and their limits, called their closed convex hulls. These mixtures permit mathematical averaging when deriving worst-case limits. It requires no distribution over physical installations.

\begin{theorem}[A complete-record test, A.G1]\label{body:strategy}
Assume the interface exhausts the pre-verdict information, both labels are possible, either terminal report is lawful, and the controller may randomize privately over complete lawful plans. The least worst-world error of an always-binary verdict is one half exactly when \(K_+\) and \(K_-\) intersect. More generally it is \((1-\gamma)/2\), where \(\gamma\) is the minimum, over a label weight and one vector from each hull, of the largest weighted absolute separation visible to any one plan. The exact formula and its attainment are in Theorem~\ref{adv:ag1}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Why mixtures matter]
Against given weighted positive and negative laws the best decision on a stopped record selects the larger of its two weighted probabilities. Summing the smaller probabilities gives its error. A finite minimax argument exchanges adversarial mixing with the auditor's private randomization. Zero separation forces equal total weights because each stopped-record law sums to one. It then forces the same probabilities on every admitted record. Conversely, a common vector with equal class weights makes even the best plan guess. One can have a common mixture even when each simple opposite pair differs somewhere.
\end{proof}
For instance, one observation is a bit with success probability either zero or one on the positive side and \(1/2\) on the negative side. Each individual positive law differs from the negative law, but the equal positive mixture equals it. This example allows just one observation. Repeated access to the same fixed coin would constitute a different design.

Independent physical facts can resolve a different obstruction. Suppose three possible installations each allows two of three actions, with allowed pairs \(\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{a,c\}\). Every pair of installations admits a common action, but the three together admit none. A report that merely confirms the same target label cannot fix that problem. If there are \(D\) deterministic complete policies, some subset of at most \(D\) worlds already witnesses infeasibility of the common randomized policy's affine requirements. A minimum safeguard selection removes at least one world from every such obstruction, while retaining the specified reference cases and the useful-service floor. Theorems~\ref{adv:ag2} and~\ref{adv:ag3} give the exact statement and a worked strategic service. Theorems~\ref{thm:t21}--\ref{thm:t24} distinguish ordinary transcript similarity, information purchased over time and validity under adaptive sampling.
